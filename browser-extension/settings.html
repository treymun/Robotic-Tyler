Phone remote

Control Tyler from your phone's browser on the same Wi-Fi. The phone shows Tyler's panel in your theme, live, with the theme's extras: start or pause him, play clips, run the Music DJ, type in chat, change the theme and more. Nothing to install. The phone never sees your keys or other settings. A paired phone can read the chat's latest 40 messages for its Chat log card (it asks only while the card is open).

The phone remote is part of the desktop app: it runs a small page on your home network, which a Chrome extension can't do.

Chat images

Friends type !image and a description (for example !image a cat in a smoke grenade, or !image best …, !image venice …); Tyler draws it and posts it in the chat as a normal Steam image. Every picture is a paid request to the style's provider, counted in your spending and stopped by the spending cap. Steam only lets the bot's account post pictures once it has spent $5 on Steam (a limited account can't); Tyler checks that before drawing, so a limited account never pays for a picture.

Styles

Three styles use your OpenRouter key; three use a Venice key and are uncensored as far as Venice allows (its own servers, safe mode off; illegal content is always blocked). Pick the model for each style; the default style is what a plain !image uses.

Theme voices

Tyler can say typed messages in each theme's character voice: G-Man for Steam, GLaDOS for Portal, the radio operator for Counter-Strike 1.6, and so on. The voices are clones made on this PC by a local engine (Qwen3-TTS), so they need no key, account or payment per message. Use them from the Theme voices card in Tyler's Steam chat panel, the phone remote, or !say in chat if you allow it below.

Theme voices are part of the desktop app: its installer sets up the voice engine on your PC. In Chrome, open Steam chat in the ROBOTIC TYLER desktop app to use them.

Checking this PC…

    Quality or Lightweight?

    Quality uses the larger model (Qwen3-TTS 1.7B). It copies each character's voice more closely and stumbles less (fewer odd pauses or misread words). It needs about 6.5 GB of free graphics memory on an NVIDIA card, or about 6 GB of free memory on the processor. Download: about 4.2 GB for the model.

    Lightweight uses the smaller model (Qwen3-TTS 0.6B). It needs about 4 GB of graphics memory on an NVIDIA card; on the processor it needs about as much memory as Quality. On the processor it's a little faster; on a graphics card the two take about as long. The voices sound a little less like the originals and odd pauses happen more often (Tyler retries a take that comes out the wrong length). Download: about 2.3 GB for the model.

    Both use the same voice engine (about 2.7 GB with graphics-card support, 0.25 GB for the processor only). You can install both and switch any time. With the standard engine, theme voices are made ahead of playing, not live: on a mid-range NVIDIA card a short line takes several seconds.

    Standard or fast engine?

    Standard is Qwen3-TTS's own code. It works on any PC, with or without a graphics card.

    Fast is faster-qwen3-tts (MIT licence): the same Qwen3-TTS models and the same 25 voices, run with CUDA graphs on an NVIDIA card. Its published figures, on an RTX 4060 laptop-class card under Windows, are about 1.8 seconds of speech made per second with Quality and 2.3 with Lightweight (the standard engine: about 0.23), with the first audio ready in under half a second. That's quick enough for Tyler to answer in a theme voice during a call (Settings → Voice → Speaking voice). It's new in Tyler and hasn't been tested on every card yet: Run a voice check after switching shows the real speed on your PC.

    Switching downloads only what's different (about 15 MB); the rest of the engine is reused. You can switch back the same way. It needs the graphics card: with Processor only Tyler uses the standard engine.

    Install

    …

    Everything is downloaded from its original source (python.org, PyPI, PyTorch and Hugging Face), checked against the SHA-256 recorded in this version of Tyler, and kept in its own folder: nothing is installed system-wide and other Python installs aren't touched. Interrupted downloads carry on where they stopped. Open the folder.
